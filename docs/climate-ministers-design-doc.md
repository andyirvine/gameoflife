# Ministers
### A co-operative game about trying to save the planet with the political capital you can actually spend

**Working title.** Sibling to Omnivex Universal Holdings, sharing its satirical DNA but pointed the other way. Omnivex is about the machine. This is about the people trying to steer it, and how little room they have to move.

**Platform.** Digital. Shared server, individual devices, playable live or asynchronously.

---

## The premise

You are a cabinet of ministers with a twenty year mandate and a planet that is already 1.3°C warmer than it should be.

You win together or you lose together. But you can be personally destroyed while the group wins, and that is the game.

Every policy that helps the planet costs you something politically. Every policy that saves your career costs the planet time. The tension is not "will you do the right thing." It is "who at this table is going to absorb the damage for it, and can they survive long enough to be useful later."

---

## The four ideas the game is built on

**1. There is no good ending.**
Only a range of bad ones, and the gap between them is enormous. You are not playing to win. You are playing to determine how many people die and whether the next generation gets a chance.

**2. Political health is a resource, not a status bar.**
Your ability to act is limited by how much your public still tolerates you. Do the right thing too often and you lose the power to keep doing it.

**3. Consequences arrive late.**
Emissions cuts do not show up in temperature for years. You will be judged in round six for a policy that saves the game in round eleven. Nothing else models the actual problem this well.

**4. Nobody agrees on what is coming.**
Every minister has a different forecast. One of them is more accurate than the others. Nobody is told which, including the minister who holds it.

## Setup

### Players
3 to 6, each taking a ministerial portfolio.

| Minister | Discounted domain | Structural weakness |
|---|---|---|
| Energy | Power generation, grid, fossil transition | Blamed first for price shocks and blackouts |
| Agriculture & Land | Forests, farming, food systems, methane | Rural base, fastest to riot |
| Treasury | Carbon pricing, subsidies, debt, tariffs | Owns every downturn, blamed for every cost |
| Foreign & Trade | Non member diplomacy, border taxes, tech transfer | No domestic base to protect them |
| Environment | Oceans, biodiversity, restoration, protected areas | Cheap wins, but perpetually accused of irrelevance |
| Civil Resilience | Adaptation, displacement, disaster response, food aid | Only visible after something has already gone wrong |

### Doctrine
At game start each minister picks a **doctrine**. This is not a restriction. It is a discount.

- **Accelerationist**: cheaper mitigation, more expensive relief
- **Guardian**: cheaper adaptation and restoration, more expensive mitigation
- **Dealmaker**: cheaper diplomacy and trade levers, more expensive domestic policy
- **Populist**: cheaper relief and dividends, more expensive everything else

Acting outside your doctrine is always allowed and always costs full price. Pivoting is expensive, not forbidden, which is how real ministerial politics works.

### Forecasts
Each minister is issued a **forecasting model** at game start. The models disagree with each other, materially, on things that matter: feedback loop timing, displacement projections, how much warming is already locked in, how fast the ice goes.

One model is significantly more accurate than the rest. Nobody is told which one, and the holder does not know either.

You can share your forecast with the cabinet. You cannot prove it. **Being right and disbelieved is a real failure state**, and it is precisely what climate science has spent forty years experiencing.

Forecast confidence intervals **narrow as the game progresses**. In round two you are making twenty year commitments on wide error bars. By round eight you know far more and have far less room left to act. That squeeze is the entire tragedy compressed into one mechanic.

### Who was right

Accuracy is **inferable during play and named explicitly at the end**.

As events resolve, reality confirms or contradicts each model. A cabinet paying attention can work out who to trust by around round eight, which is precisely when the cheap decisions are gone. Credibility is earned late and spent under pressure.

Then the epilogue says it out loud:

> *Civil Resilience held the accurate model. Their displacement projections were correct from round two. They fell in round nine.*

Being right, unprovable, and destroyed for it is a complete arc, and the game should be willing to write it down.

## The round

Twelve rounds, roughly two years each.

### Phase 1: The world moves
Two decks resolve. They do different jobs and should feel different.

**World Deck. Responsive. Political and economic.**
Draws are weighted by the current state of the game, not a fixed schedule. Non member states defect, free ride, or open back channels. Elections turn. Coalitions fracture. Summits achieve nothing. Industry relocates. A tipping point warning turns out to have been a modelling error, and gets used for the next decade to discredit everything else.

If your coalition is fracturing, the deck gets nastier. If you are stable and credible, opportunities appear. The world reacts to you.

**Events Deck. Random. Physical. Weighted by planetary state.**
Feedback loops and shocks. Siberian methane release. AMOC disruption. Amazon dieback. West Antarctic collapse. Monsoon failure. Wet bulb events. Regional crop failure.

These are **probability weighted by the state of the planet, not flatly random.** At +1.4°C the methane card sits in the deck at low odds. At +1.8°C it is drawn three times as often.

This produces the thing that makes climate risk psychologically real: **your choices change the odds, never the possibility.** You can play well and still draw the card that ends you. You can play badly and get away with it. The game must allow both, because reality does, and it is why the argument never resolves.

Feedback loop cards do the properly horrifying thing: they **modify the pipeline retroactively**. A methane release does not just add emissions, it shortens everyone's remaining lag windows. Policies you placed at lag 5 now arrive too late to matter. Years of work invalidated by geology.

### Phase 2: Budget
Each minister receives points equal to their political health tier.

| Political health | Points per round |
|---|---|
| Strong (8 to 10) | 5 |
| Stable (5 to 7) | 4 |
| Fragile (3 to 4) | 3 |
| Critical (1 to 2) | 2 |
| Fallen (0) | Succession. See below. |

### Phase 3: Negotiation
Open cabinet channel plus private minister to minister channels.

The private channels are where the game actually happens. *"One of us has to take the methane hit. It cannot be me, I am at 3."* That conversation, held privately while the rest of the cabinet waits, is the whole design working.

Deals struck here are not binding. Nothing is enforced. That matters later.

### Phase 4: Commitment
Policies selected privately, revealed simultaneously. Timed.

You will get rounds where three ministers dumped everything into the same lever and nobody covered agriculture. Good.

### Phase 5: Resolution
Policies resolve. Political health adjusts. Planetary effects go onto the pipeline.

### Phase 6: Consequence
Everything scheduled to land this round lands now, including policies enacted five rounds ago by ministers who no longer hold office and were destroyed for enacting them.

## The pipeline

The mechanic that makes this a climate game rather than a resource allocation puzzle.

Enacting a policy places its planetary effect on a track:

- **Lag 1.** Relief, dividends, emergency aid, adaptation. Fast, visible, politically useful, does nothing for the long game.
- **Lag 2 to 3.** Carbon pricing, subsidy removal, methane action, deforestation bans. Meaningful.
- **Lag 4 to 6.** Renewables buildout, grid infrastructure, restoration, structural transition. The ones that decide the ending.

Political cost is paid **immediately**. Planetary benefit arrives **later**.

You will lose ministers over policies that had not yet paid off. That is not a bug.

---

## Severity

Every policy has three tiers, and the relationship between cost and pain is deliberately non linear.

**Example: methane action**

| Tier | Policy | Planetary effect | Political cost |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Fossil infrastructure leak detection and repair | Modest cut, lag 2 | Almost none. Close to a free win. |
| Moderate | Add landfill capture and subsidised livestock feed additives | Real cut, lag 2 | Low. Farmers grumble. |
| Aggressive | Mandatory herd caps and enforced livestock reduction | Largest fast acting lever in the game, lag 2 | Severe. Agriculture takes heavy damage, likely cascade to allied portfolios. |

Light options are consistently underpriced and undervalued by players. Let them discover that on their own.

---

## Political health

### Losing it
- Policy that raises consumer prices
- Policy that removes livelihoods
- Being in office when a shock hits your domain
- Being visibly associated with another minister's unpopular win

### Regaining it
- Relief, dividend and adaptation policies. These trade planetary progress for political recovery, and that trade should feel bad every single time.
- Pipeline effects landing visibly and being correctly attributed to you
- Time, slowly, if you spend a round doing nothing
- An event landing well in your domain

The recovery valve is essential. Without it the game becomes a death spiral where somebody tanks in round four and spectates for the rest of the evening.

---

## Succession

**Nobody leaves the table. Ever.**

At zero political health you do not lose your seat. You lose your *self*. Next round you are still the Energy Minister. Just not the same one.

Same portfolio, same domain, same locked in policies you inherited from yourself. By round eight a player has spent hours thinking about grid transition and knows where the bodies are buried. Throwing that away punishes the player rather than the character, and makes the whole cabinet dumber.

### Your successor is the shape of your failure

How you fell determines who replaces you.

| How you fell | Who arrives | What they cannot do |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel price anger | Populist | Cannot raise consumer energy costs for 3 rounds |
| Unprepared for a disaster in your domain | Technocrat | Cheap adaptation, starts at health 3 instead of 4 |
| Seen doing the coalition's dirty work | Nationalist | Cannot spend on anything international |
| Industry collapse or job losses | Protectionist | Cannot enact anything that touches employment |

This bakes the cabinet's collective mistakes into its future capability. A cabinet that has burned three ministers on price shocks ends up staffed with people who structurally cannot touch prices, arriving exactly when pricing is the only lever left.

### What succession costs

- **One transition round at reduced points.** Governments do not function during a handover.
- **Every informal deal your predecessor made is void.** Locked in policy survives. Promises do not. If Treasury agreed to cover you next round, the new you has no such agreement, and Treasury is free to deny it ever existed.
- **The restricted menu.** Not fewer points. Specific tools removed, at the moment you most need them.

### The files

The successor opens the office files and reads their predecessor's private forecast.

Sometimes that is a gift.

Sometimes it is the discovery that the person you replaced saw the methane risk correctly in round four, said so, was not believed, pushed anyway, and was destroyed for it. You are now sitting in their chair holding proof they were right.

Nobody watches. Everybody keeps paying.

---

## The ratchet

Some policies **lock in**. Once enacted they cannot be reversed by any minister, including a successor. Infrastructure, price signals, protected areas, binding treaties.

Others **decay** if unmaintained, costing upkeep points every round.

Deciding what to make permanent while you still have the power to do it is one of the sharpest strategic questions in the game. It is also the most satisfying thing to leave behind for a successor who hates you.

---

## The counter

One number. Above every dashboard. Every round. Visible to everyone.

**Cumulative excess deaths attributable to climate impacts.**

It only ever goes up. There is no policy, no doctrine, no perfect round that makes it stop. The best available cabinet in the best available world still watches it climb, because people are already dying and more of them will.

All you can do is change the slope.

That is the honest position and the game should refuse to soften it. No projections, no counterfactuals, no "lives saved" counter sitting next to it offering comfort. A single figure, always rising, always in your peripheral vision while you argue about tariffs.

It will drive more careful play than any political health bar ever could.

## Endings

There is no win screen. There is a **world state report** and a **ledger of what you traded**.

> **2046.** Emissions at 19 GtCO₂e. Temperature peaked at 1.71°C in 2041 and is now falling. Forest loss halted in 2038.
>
> Cumulative excess deaths attributable to climate impacts, 2026 to 2046: **1.4 billion**.
> Displaced and not resettled: 310 million.
> Coalition states that abandoned democratic governance during the mandate: 3.
>
> Three ministers fell. Two were right.
>
> You held the line on emissions by accepting a food crisis you had the resources to prevent in Round 7 and chose not to.

That last line is the point. The game names the specific round where you traded lives for trajectory, because you did, and you knew at the time.

### Designing against nihilism

If nothing works, players disengage after one session. The counter is that **the gap between outcomes has to be enormous even though none of them are good.**

Best available might be 400 million dead and a stabilised climate that keeps falling. Worst available is 4 billion and a runaway that nobody can stop. Agency is completely preserved. Victory simply is not on the menu.

That distinction has to be legible by round three, not just in the epilogue, or players will assume their choices are cosmetic and stop caring.

### Planetary targets

Assessed at the final round. Hitting most produces a survivable ending. Hitting few does not.

| Target | Baseline | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| Annual emissions | 40 GtCO₂e | 18 or below |
| Peak temperature | +1.3°C | Peaks below +1.6°C and falling |
| Tropical forest loss | 10M ha per year | Net zero loss |
| Wetland and mangrove restoration | Baseline | +30% |
| Biodiversity index | Falling | Stabilised |
| Coral reef extent | Falling | Under 30% total loss |
| Climate displacement | 30M per year | Growth flattened, under 40M |
| Food insecurity | 735M | 600M or below |
| Climate damages | 2% of GDP | 3% of GDP or below |

### Individual ending
You survive with your portfolio intact and your name on something that worked.

Both can happen. Usually one does.

## Opening state

**The game starts wherever the planet actually is.** Live data, loaded at session start.

| Metric | Source |
|---|---|
| CO₂ concentration | Scripps / NOAA, Mauna Loa |
| Temperature anomaly | Copernicus or Berkeley Earth |
| Annual emissions | Global Carbon Project |
| Arctic sea ice | NSIDC |
| Forest loss | Global Forest Watch |

Most publish annually or monthly, which suits a game that loads state at session start rather than streaming.

Three things this buys you beyond realism.

**The game gets harder every year it exists.** A 2029 playthrough starts from a worse position than a 2026 one, with less room and a hotter Events Deck. Nobody has to design that in. It just happens. It is the most damning thing this design is capable of doing.

**It kills the solved opening.** The state drifts, so the round one calculus drifts with it. There is no memorised optimal first move.

**It removes the argument.** Nobody can dismiss the starting conditions as pessimistic authorial framing. It is just the readings.

### What it breaks

You cannot balance test a game whose starting conditions move under you. So there are two modes.

**Live mode** is the default and how the game is meant to be played.

**Canonical 2026** is a frozen scenario for tuning, for tutorials, and for comparing playthroughs against a fixed benchmark.

Any cross session scoring has to be normalised against the starting state, or a 2027 cabinet looks incompetent next to a 2026 one for no reason at all.

---

## Digital implementation

Shared server, individual devices, live or asynchronous.

**What the platform unlocks:**
- Hidden commitment with a timer, where the timer itself is pressure
- Per minister dashboards showing only your forecast and your domain's detail
- Automated pipeline and metric tracking, which would have been miserable on a table
- Asynchronous play. A round a day over two weeks. Between rounds people actually think, negotiate, and change their minds, which is far closer to how cabinets work than a two hour sitting.
- Open cabinet channel for public argument, private DMs for the sacrifice negotiations

The private DM channel may be the single most important feature in the build. Everything uncomfortable about this game happens there.

---

## Tone

Satirical, but the satire points at the system, not the people. The ministers are trying. The advisors are honest. The absurdity lives in the World Deck: the free riding petrostate, the summit that achieves nothing, the tipping point warning that turns out to be a modelling error and poisons the well for a decade.

Players should finish having felt the specific frustration of knowing exactly what needs to happen and not being able to afford it.

If someone stands up at the end and says "we did everything right and it still was not enough," the game worked.

---

## Settled

- **No good ending.** A world state report and a ledger of what you traded, not a score.
- **The counter.** One figure, always rising, always visible.
- **Forecasts.** Private, contradictory, one materially accurate. Inferable during play, named at the end.
- **Two decks.** World Deck responsive to game state, Events Deck weighted by planetary state.
- **Twelve rounds.** No fixed escalation schedule.
- **Succession, not elimination.** Same seat, new person, shaped by how you fell.
- **Six players maximum.**
- **Live planetary data as the opening state**, with a frozen canonical scenario for tuning.
- **No asymmetric variant.** Not now. Possibly never. It would break the frame the game depends on.

---

## Still open

1. **How is the death toll actually calculated?** It has to be traceable enough that a player can look at round seven and understand what it cost, without becoming a spreadsheet they optimise coldly. This is the hardest remaining design problem and probably the one to prototype first.
2. **Does an unlucky Events Deck draw need a floor?** If a cabinet plays well and draws the methane card in round three, is that a legitimate ending or a feel-bad that kills replay? Argument either way, and it is a playtest question rather than a whiteboard one.
3. **What does the second playthrough feel like?** Live opening state helps. It may not be enough on its own. Rotating doctrines or randomised portfolio weaknesses might be needed.
4. **How long is a live round in practice?** Asynchronous play at a round a day gives twelve days, which is a long commitment to ask for before anyone knows if they like it.
